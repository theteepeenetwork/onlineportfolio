A house style for the activities we publish

Every template StoryJar puts in the shared library is built from the same small kit of parts, in the same colours, so the library reads as one considered thing rather than fifteen worksheets by fifteen hands. This is our internal standard for our own content. It is not a scheme for schools, and nothing here is ever recommended, enforced or suggested to a teacher.

Colours are the live tokens from src/app/globals.css. Every text pairing below has been checked against WCAG 2.2 AA. Pages are drawn at the canvas model space of 1000 by 700, so the mockups double as the geometry for the objects payload.

1. Our house palette

StoryJar already has a warm, hand-inked palette. Inside our own library we use it consistently rather than decoratively: one colour, one job, across every activity we author. That consistency is worth having because a teacher browsing forty StoryJar activities should feel one hand behind them.

Scope: this is ours, and it stays ours. Schools have their own established systems for scaffolding, challenge levels, working walls and success criteria, built over years and shared with parents. Ours is a house style for the content we publish, in the same way a publisher's books look like each other. A teacher building their own template picks whatever colours their school already uses, and StoryJar stays quiet about it. Section 7 sets out exactly where that line sits.

The one rule that carries all of this. Apparatus colours are spoken for by the maths kit: blue ones, teal tens, honey hundreds, jam thousands, red and yellow two-colour counters. Template furniture uses the tints, never the solids, so a hint box can never be mistaken for a counter and a counter never reads as a hint.

2. The furniture kit

Eight parts, built from shapes and text objects the canvas already has. Nothing here needs new code: every one is a rounded rectangle, a line and a label, locked so a child cannot nudge it out of place.

All eight, at real scale. Each is a locked shape plus locked text in the objects payload. The outline is always ink at 4 units, the corner radius is always generous, and nothing sits closer than 20 units to the page edge so a thumb resting on the tablet never covers a hint.

3. Three templates, fully dressed

Feed the teddy · EYFS · ten frame

Reading order without reading. The reminder sits top left where a left-to-right eye starts, the numbered step is the only jam thing at the top so it wins attention, the apparatus tray and the hint sit low where a thumb rests, and the ten frame is the emptiest, brightest object on the page. A child who cannot read a word still knows where to look and what to touch.

Number line hops · KS1 · addition

The worked example is the scaffold. One question already answered, in honey, so a child sees the method rather than being told it. The word bank is the bit teachers will notice: it turns "explain your answer" from an impossible ask into a fill-in for a child with limited English or limited confidence.

The fraction wall detective · KS2 · equivalence

Auto-marked and hand-worked on one page. The teal-bordered boxes are the quiz layer, which stays interactive and marks itself. Everything else is drawn on and flattened into the child's work. The sentence starters are what turn the written half from a blank page into something a Year 4 will actually attempt.

4. The scaffolding ladder

The same template, published three times at three levels of support. A teacher assigns the rung their group needs, and the fading is visible on the page rather than hidden in a setting. It also means one piece of design work yields three library entries.

Rung one, full support. Worked example, reminder, word bank, success criteria. For the group who need to see it done before they can do it.

Rung two, partial. The worked example goes. The rule and the words stay, and a work zone appears, because the child now has something of their own to say.

Rung three, open. A prompt, the apparatus, and space. The support has gone and the challenge has grown, which is the difference between fading a scaffold and just removing help.

5. Writing for a child to read

Instruction wording is where most classroom software quietly fails. These are the rules the library copy follows, tightened by band.

Instead of this

Using the number line provided, demonstrate your understanding of addition by representing the calculation 8 + 5.

Consider which resources would be most appropriate before beginning.

Ensure you have checked your work carefully.

Write this

Show me 8 add 5.

Pick the hops you need.

Count your hops again.

  • Verb first, one action per line. "Draw", "Count", "Drag", "Tell me". A child scanning a page finds the verb before they find the sentence.
  • Eight words or fewer for EYFS and KS1. Twelve for KS2. Longer than that and it becomes a reading task wearing a maths costume.
  • Speak to one child. "Show me" rather than "pupils should show". It is their jar.
  • Say what finished looks like. The success strip is the whole of assessment for learning that a seven-year-old can operate on their own.
  • Every instruction survives being read aloud, because in EYFS it will be. Read each one out before it is published, and cut anything that makes you take a breath in the middle.
  • Pair a word with a picture wherever the word carries the meaning. Microphone icon beside "tell me", hop icon beside "hops". Picture and word together teaches the word.
Atkinson Hyperlegible is already the body font. It was designed for low vision readers and its letterforms are unusually distinct, which is a real advantage for early readers and dyslexic children. That is a genuine accessibility claim StoryJar can make and most competitors cannot, and it costs nothing because the decision is already taken.
Things to keep off a template. Fill patterns and gradients, since the child draws on top and needs contrast. More than two accent colours on one page. Text below 18 units in model space. Anything in the four corners, where hands rest. And red or green as a right-or-wrong signal, both because of colour blindness and because green is being kept back for a correctness cue that does not exist yet.

6. Where the line sits with schools

The distinction matters enough to write down, because it decides several product choices and a good deal of how we talk about the library.

What we doWhat we leave alone
Apply the house style to activities StoryJar authors and publishes, so the library is coherent. Any expectation that a teacher's own templates follow it. Their template, their school's colours.
Give a teacher who copies a library activity a full, editable copy, colours and all, to change freely. Locking, watermarking or restyling a copied activity back towards our palette.
Offer a furniture group in the builder as plain, neutral, recolourable shapes. Naming them after our meanings or defaulting them to our tints, which would read as a rule.
Keep the meanings in this document, for whoever authors our library content. Putting the meanings in the product: no legend, no tooltip, no "recommended colour" nudge.
Talk publicly about what the canvas can do: hints, reminders, word banks, fading support. Marketing a StoryJar colour code, or implying schools should adopt one.
The practical reason, beyond good manners. A school running a chilli challenge system, a traffic light self-assessment, or its own colour-coded working wall would find our meanings actively conflicting with what its children have already learned. Pushing a scheme into that would be worse than useless: it would make StoryJar the thing a teacher has to work around. Consistency in our own content costs a school nothing. A scheme aimed at their practice costs them something real.

7. What this changes about the build

All of it is data. Every box above is a locked rounded rectangle plus a locked text object in objectsJson, so a template that looks designed costs the same as one that does not. The work is deciding the constants once, here, and then reusing them fifteen times.

  • Outline: ink #22304a, stroke width 4 in model units, corner radius 18.
  • Body text: 20 to 21 units. Headings inside a box: 23 to 27, Fredoka weight 600.
  • Padding inside a box: 22 units. Gap between boxes: 22 units. Page margin: 30.
  • Boxes sit in bands: support along the top, the thing being worked on in the middle, tools and words along the bottom where hands are.

Two small pieces of product work would make these templates noticeably better, and both are worth scoping separately. A read-aloud button on activity instructions, which is what makes the EYFS band self-serve. And a furniture group in the template builder's shape kit, so anybody can drop a rounded box with a label in one tap rather than building it from a rectangle and a text object.

That second one needs care. The shapes should arrive neutral and be recoloured in a tap, named for their form rather than our meanings, so a teacher fits them to whatever their school already uses. We get the same speed benefit when we author library content, and a teacher gets a better box, with no suggestion attached about what colour it ought to be.
